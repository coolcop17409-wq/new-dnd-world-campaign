# Institutions wilderness and local protection
Everyday institutions include merchant houses, guilds, temples, courts, schools, workshops, and neighborhood associations. Their cross-border networks have regional strengths and independent interests, but remain subject to local law, patronage, and political pressure. Local leaders and branches are undeveloped; illustrative disputes are not fixed events.
## Cross border institutions
Agreements, reputation, introductions, and shared interests support these networks. Coverage is uneven and recommendations confer no foreign legal privileges. Veyr monitors contacts; the Dominion and Vhalzareth require permission or powerful sponsorship.
### The Serean Houses
The Serean Houses are cooperating merchant firms rather than a centrally governed league, sovereign power, or trading monopoly. Their reciprocal arrangements strongly connect Talaris, Merovar, Veyport, Selwick, Southwatch, and Dawnrest, with selected partners in Ashcrown and Thornveil.
The Serean Houses offer shipping, warehousing, exchange, introductions, and client credit. Letters, witnessed accounts, kin partnerships, and agents' reputations support payment across ports without carrying full purchase prices, as well as lodging and onward passage. Local courts, arbitration, withheld credit, and refusal of business enforce agreements; firms have no independent arrest powers.
War, confiscation, debt, and patronage constrain operations. Firms disagree over embargoes, slavery, wartime contracts, and protection of threatened partners. Commercial cooperation does not create political unity or moral consistency.
### The Correspondence
The Correspondence links independent magical teachers, schools, and libraries through a loose tradition of scholarly exchange. It has no central academy governing magic.
| Practice | Purpose and limit |
| - | - |
| Introductions | Help students locate teachers; admission depends on resources, reputation, and permission |
| Copied manuscripts | Preserve and circulate knowledge through slow, costly, error-prone copying |
| Visiting instruction | Provides specialist teaching under terms patrons may restrict |
| Witnessed demonstrations | Establish claims and prestige before peers, without universal legal authority |
| Shared warnings | Report dangerous techniques, fraud, or missing scholars; allegations can be mistaken or malicious |
Participants dispute secrecy, fees, dangerous research, and duties to rulers. Some share practical knowledge broadly; others exchange only with trusted peers. Veyr seeks access while monitoring contacts; other courts recruit teachers and seek exclusive discoveries.
### The Open Hearths
Open Hearth hospices independently manage local funding and obligations while recognizing attendants, training, and recommendations from other houses. Temple, civic, and private foundations participate without a common divine patron.
Attendants provide rest, food, wound care, midwifery, and traveler assistance, train apprentices, and exchange knowledge. Limited beds, supplies, and personnel constrain care; magical treatment requires a suitable practitioner.
Their shared pledge offers immediate basic care without conversion or proof of citizenship. It guarantees neither indefinite accommodation nor immunity from arrest, and resources cannot meet every need. Governments may demand military priority, patient records, or surrender of fugitives. Houses differ in their resistance.
### Courier partnerships
There is no worldwide postal service. Partnerships connect riders, shipmasters, inns, temples, estates, and trading establishments along particular routes; firms remain unnamed until locally relevant.
| Method | Use and limit |
| - | - |
| Merchant carriage | Letters accompany ships and caravans; departures dictate timing |
| Hired couriers | Expensive urgent delivery to particular destinations |
| Local relays | Exchanges along selected roads under regionally varying agreements |
| Official dispatches | Privileged state and military routes, often closed to private clients |
| Magical messages | Scarce practitioners serve important institutions or paying clients within limited capacity |
Seals, trusted intermediaries, duplicate letters, and agreed phrases help protect correspondence but guarantee neither secrecy nor arrival. News follows the same routes, acquiring omissions, exaggeration, and delays. A harbor may receive overseas news before its inland government does.
### Keepers of the Last Road
The Keepers of the Last Road are a funerary fellowship serving travelers and communities far from ancestral burial places. Lay attendants, grave tenders, scribes, and priests from different traditions identify the dead, preserve names and possessions, arrange burial, notify survivors, and maintain records.
They respect known wishes and local customs within available means. Their testimony can support inheritance and remembrance, challenge casualty reports, expose unacknowledged deaths, or help someone falsely declared dead recover standing. They maintain graves and seek assistance against desecration, curses, or undead.
The Keepers require no particular divine allegiance and claim no control over the afterlife. Governments can restrict records; notification relies on existing religious, courier, and merchant contacts. In Orsaven they tend a burial hillside with older galleries, service grilles and access shafts. Their local grave tenders and apprentices can arrange cordons and specialist repair after a collapse, seek outside rescue assistance, and discuss funded work with affected vault families; their means do not guarantee a reward or an open-ended commission. Played rescue outcomes belong in the Continuity Pack.
Protection is concentrated around productive land, settlements, and routes; remote communities rely more heavily on local arrangements.
## Wilderness inhabitants and supernatural places
Land beyond state control is often inhabited and claimed. Royal maps may call it wilderness while residents recognize boundaries, histories, and obligations. Fey communities, giant holds, independent dragons, undead sites, and Scarlands are numerous, generally small, and dispersed. They receive no separate territories or labels on the world political map; particular examples may appear on regional or local maps. Individual names and locations remain open.
| Presence | Local power and limits |
| - | - |
| Fey courts and communities | Particular forests, waters, crossings, and seasonal places are held through magic and negotiated customs. Disturbing agreements can endanger neighboring settlements; locating a court does not guarantee a battle can be forced. |
| Giant holds | Strongholds, grazing grounds, hunting territories, and approaches combine formidable defenders with difficult terrain. Some trade, others raid; conquest can cost more than negotiated access. |
| Independent dragons | Claim lairs, feeding grounds, treasure sites, or tribute. They can devastate exposed holdings without defending every acre. Their conduct varies, including bounded agreements and avoidance. |
| Undead domains | Persist around ruins, battlefields, tombs, or particular undead rulers. Destroying visible forces may leave the source intact. |
| Scarlands | Local persistent Convergence conditions demand knowledge and preparation rather than simply a larger force. |
Ordinary villages, mobile peoples, hunters, and other independent communities also inhabit these lands. Not every forest has a supernatural sovereign, and fey interests extend beyond misleading travelers.
### Local relationships
Settlements negotiate gathering, grazing, and crossing rights; trade food, materials, crafts, and expertise; pay tribute or provide service under coercion; observe seasonal closures and marked boundaries; cooperate against shared threats; or endure recurring raids, encroachment, and retaliation.
Relations follow particular histories rather than species alone. Agreements can outlast rulers but falter through migration, expanding estates, war, or the death of a key participant. Local guides know whose permission matters and which customs or circumstances have changed.
### Abandoned and reclaimed places
Abandoned farms can result from raids, disease, flooding, or failed protection. Ruined settlements may lack supplies or useful routes, or remain cursed. Battlefields and burial grounds can contain undead, unresolved obligations, or dangerous surviving magical workings. Unstable Convergence conditions can limit residence. Forest, marsh, and new inhabitants can reclaim country still claimed by former rulers.
Abandonment does not mean ownerlessness: heirs, descendants, ancestral claims, and shared obligations may survive. Some ruins become quarries, pasture, shrines, shelters, or new settlements. Others remain because recovery exceeds available resources. None of these patterns establishes a particular site or encounter.
### Creatures and persistent hazards
Territorial creatures have ranges, nesting grounds, and warning signs. Migration affects grazing, fishing, roads, and watches. Livestock, refuse, and disturbed habitat can attract predators. Most creatures need food, shelter, and suitable conditions; supernatural exceptions require particular explanations. Intelligent beings pursue beliefs and material interests as well as territory or feeding.
Undead, curses, and supernatural infestations require understanding their sources. Communities use barriers, guarded herds, seasonal routes, refuges, warnings, negotiated access, and specialists. Adventurers are useful where established arrangements fail or threats exceed local capability. War and opportunism worsen hazards; the Convergence happened once, with new interdimensional arrivals rare exceptions.
### Scarland conditions
Each Scarland has a particular persistent condition rather than arbitrary changing magic. Its reach is local, though consequences can affect neighbors and routes. Experience reveals useful patterns of timing, equipment, and avoidance. Some support livelihoods or managed passage without continual catastrophe.
Precautions can work even where causes remain disputed. The established road with differing travel times by direction fits this pattern; its location, precise behavior, and explanation remain open. Other Scarlands need not involve time or distance. Additional individual conditions remain undeveloped.
### Maintaining protection
Safety depends on maintained barriers, watches, refuges, escorts, avoidance, tribute, specialists, and evacuation. Protection grants benefits and leverage; its withdrawal can unravel established security.
Households maintain basic defenses and alarms; communities organize watches and militia; local authorities support trained defenders; regional governments reinforce larger threats; hired specialists address particular gaps. Funding comes from dues, tolls, taxes, labor obligations and contracts. Failed protection can provoke flight, withheld payments or new patrons.
| Force | Role and constraint |
| - | - |
| Militia | Local defense and evacuation; members must return to work |
| Town guards | Gates, streets, markets and arrests; limited countryside reach |
| Household retainers | Serve patrons whose interests may differ from the community's |
| Garrisons | Defend fixed positions; reassignment exposes them |
| Campaign armies | Require assembly, provisions, transport and money |
| Hired companies | Vary in skill; depend on contracts, payment and reputation |
The empires have larger permanent military cores but remain constrained by supply and occupation. Local forces adapt to recurring monsters. Billeting, requisition and damaged farmland can make armies burdensome even to those they protect.
# Adventurers law and the Wardens
Adventurer is a social description, not a universal legal status. Permission to do a job, authority to use force, and ownership of recovered property are distinct. Employers can only grant rights they possess. Local custom, contracts, commissions, warrants and negotiated access determine permission.
Routine work has understood terms; not every job needs royal paperwork. Known owners retain claims, tombs and ruins can have ancestral rights, salvage may earn compensation without ownership, and apparent ownerless goods follow local law. Self-defense does not authorize unlimited retaliation or coercion.
Legal authority, practical acceptance and moral justification may differ, especially under occupation. Ambiguity should support meaningful choices rather than constant administrative obstacles. There is no universal adventurers' guild.
The Wardens of Tyr originated under Tyr; Tyr is alive but has withdrawn from directing them, so members make operational decisions. Their principle is that power does not place someone beyond judgment. Wardens investigate serious magical homicide, coercion, impersonation, dangerous summoning, terrorism, powerful rogue practitioners, forbidden planar work, fragment trafficking, cross-border offenses, and supernatural incidents. Full commission requires voluntarily and irreversibly sacrificing one natural hand; a trainee may serve without this sacrifice but is not fully commissioned. The removed hand is replaced by an arcane prosthesis, the Hand of Tyr, which serves as identification, proof of commission, forensic instrument, professional symbol, and specialized equipment. It is permanently bound to its Warden and becomes inert when removed or when its owner dies; theft does not transfer its use. Each Hand contains a unique magical identity and commission; Wardens authenticate one another by clasping Hands. Through touch, the Hand gives trained users sensations about magical disturbances, locations, creatures, objects, enchantments, signatures, wards, and contamination. Interpretation is fallible; it is professional equipment, not a major combat weapon. A dead Warden's Hand is traditionally recovered; its identity is lost, but its enchanted material can be reforged into later Hands. Treaties recognize commissions with different powers in each state; Wardens do not replace local enforcement or hold universal pursuit, arrest, or confiscation rights. Evidence, authority, resources, and local cooperation limit their reach.
# Alderbridge reference district
Alderbridge is an adopted reference district in Oathmark, away from the immediate imperial frontier. It is not the selected campaign start. Working population is about 6,000 across a market town of roughly 1,200, eight villages, and scattered farms.
| Place | Function |
| - | - |
| Alderbridge | Bridge, river landing, mills, workshops, warehouses and small court |
| Eight villages | Grain, livestock, timber and local necessities; most within a day's travel of town |
| Watch Hill | Fortified refuge and small garrison above the main road |
| Old upland road | Route through wooded hills and abandoned settlements |
| Marsh margin | Fishing, reeds and seasonal grazing; local knowledge matters |
A district representative serves in Oathmark's assembly; the local council allocates road, bridge, refuge and defense contributions. Villages retain customary rights. Town guards, a small permanent garrison, watches and limited militia obligations provide protection. Exact force sizes remain open.
Storm damage to the bridge, missing livestock and an armed group charging disputed protection tolls are example hooks, not fixed events. They illustrate competing demands on finite defenders and the need for specific permissions when hiring outsiders.
