# Languages literacy and translation
## Common and regional languages
Common spread after the Convergence as a trade language based on a widespread native human language. Ports, markets, and diplomacy extended its use among arriving peoples. Over 1,200 years it absorbed vocabulary from other languages and developed regional varieties. The exact origin language and regional names remain open.
Common follows contact: merchants, sailors, officials, and travelers often speak it, while isolated communities may have few fluent speakers. It is generally useful in major towns and established routes without replacing local languages in households, worship, storytelling, government, and community affairs. Guides and interpreters provide nuance, trust, and access. Spoken fluency and literacy are separate.

Language does not establish species or allegiance. Upbringing, residence, and study enable learning; an Orc speaker may oppose Gruumsh and a Draconic speaker need not belong to the compact.
Veyr imposes Primordial through schools, official training, and service. Fluency marks belonging and enables advancement without guaranteeing citizenship. Local languages persist under varying pressure; Common remains useful for foreign trade, diplomacy, and expeditions. Why the transplanted human civilization adopted Primordial remains unresolved, with no elemental ancestry or allegiance implied. Its precise historical relationship to Common remains open.
Undercommon spread through drow trade, domination, and contact, then developed distinctive grammar, pronunciation, and vocabulary among deep gnomes, duergar, goblins, quaggoths, and others. It supplements native languages. Drow retain their own Elvish variety; knowledge of Elvish helps learning Undercommon without guaranteeing fluency. Its precise relationship to formal drow speech remains open. Underdark is the region's name, not the language.
## Literacy and writing
Literacy varies with occupation, education, and place. Merchants, officials, clergy, and trained craftspeople often read what work requires; others rely on memory, witnesses, and scribes. Reading, writing, and numeracy are distinct skills. A competent account keeper may struggle with court language; memorized passages do not establish fluent reading.
Languages may share a script without mutual intelligibility. Undercommon commonly uses Elvish script. Other scripts remain unspecified. Charters, contracts, correspondence, and records matter, but handwriting, terminology, and regional conventions complicate interpretation.
Proclamations are read aloud, signs use recognizable symbols, and testimony can establish claims without writing. Households, apprenticeships, temples, and schools teach different levels of literacy and numeracy. Schooling and access to scribes can protect rights or create dependence; Veyran Primordial instruction also enforces ideology.
## Translation magic
Translation follows the particular spell or effect and campaign magic rules. Literal understanding does not supply cultural knowledge, customary obligations, or recognition of allusions. Comprehension need not permit a reply. Language understanding does not automatically decipher codes or concealed meanings.
Suitable practitioners and finite magical reserves limit availability; payment, patronage, and other obligations affect access. Learned interpreters remain useful for negotiation, trust, and explaining unfamiliar institutions even with magical assistance.
# Magic everyday society and trade
The ordinary baseline is classic medieval fantasy: hand-copied manuscripts; skilled workshops; smithies; watermills and windmills; markets, merchant houses, moneylending and inconsistent measures; animal transport; river and coastal shipping; dirt roads, ferries, scattered stone bridges, wells and local irrigation. There is no gunpowder or widespread mechanization. Exceptional local engineering does not define every town.
Over 1,200 years, magical knowledge, crafts, artifacts and institutions improved unevenly. All mortal magic requires finite reserves, including minor effects. Powerful items also exhaust their reserves: minor reusable items recover slowly, powerful equipment requires specialists, materials or facilities to recharge, and consumables are expended. Magical industry is possible but limited by cost, supply and recharge infrastructure. Divine fragments are not automatically batteries. PC mechanics remain a separate established exception.
Ordinary care and skilled work sustain villages, sometimes aided by a local or visiting practitioner. Towns, cities, and major institutions support progressively rarer specialists, whose fields and availability differ. Wealth cannot guarantee immediate access. Standard magical healing potions do not exist. Ordinary mortal Clerics of a dead god lose divine spellcasting; player-character Clerics and Paladins may draw on their own remaining divinity as specified in the Continuity Pack's `RULES` tab.

### Homing-bird courier services — Day172 OOC canon ruling
Nonmagical carrier/homing pigeons exist in this medieval world. Some major cities, ports, fortified estates, military posts and commercial houses maintain trained destination lofts and negotiated courier partnerships; coverage is patchy, not every village or overseas port has a service. A mundane homing pigeon flies to its own home loft, NOT any arbitrary chosen person or address; it must first be carried to its dispatch point and is generally one-way, so return correspondence requires a different bird trained to the sending city's loft or another transport. Multiple relay legs require destination-homed birds pre-positioned at each stop, not a single bird making arbitrary stops. No automatic delivery/ownership/cost/available pairing without establishing actual route.
Ordinary trained pigeon in good weather averages approximately40–55 miles per flying hour, mostly by day; practical routine segments about50–300mi; longer flights approaching500mi possible for trained birds but not reliably at whim, and fog/storms, raptors and open sea can force delay or loss.
