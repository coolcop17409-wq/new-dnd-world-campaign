# World foundations
Ordinary mortal species cannot reproduce across species boundaries: humans with elves or orcs, distinct goblinoid species and other humanoid species are mutually incompatible; naturally born half-elves/half-orcs do not occur and fertility blessings cannot bypass this. SINGLE PLAYER-ESTABLISHED EXCEPTION: V's original, unknown ancient humanoid species could reproduce with any humanoid species; V is now its last surviving full member (not an elf). This does not let other humanoid species breed with one another, establish any surviving descendants, or grant offspring V's original species/mechanics automatically. Divine parentage is separate from these rules.
Multiethnic communities contain distinct peoples. Cross-species relationships, marriage, adoption, and cultural assimilation remain possible; Dominion replacement occurs through settlement, growth within compatible populations, and displacement. Cultural, national, clan, and regional differences alone do not establish separate species.
Fenar's direct Raukhar line inherited feline Shifter traits through a distant child of Nobanion and a dwarven line historically affected by lycanthropy; this is not cross-species reproduction or full lycanthropy. Her god-kin exception applies to this bloodline, not all Shifters.
V and Cirren are direct children of gods; their divine parents' deaths caused their Severing (identity and memory retained; supernatural ability collapsed to level1). Fenar Raukhar is a narrow hereditary exception: an unusually potent descendant of Nobanion, slain roughly six months before Day171; she also experienced the Severing. Other Raukhar relatives retain feline features and lesser traits but are not automatically demigods. Each character's recovery uses normal milestone progression; Fenar has NO celestial lion transformation before or after the Severing. Nobanion's killer and specific divine circumstances are unknown; V/Cirren parent identities remain open. Divine ancestry does not override the usual species reproduction restrictions except the explicitly defined V species.
The Convergence occurred approximately 1,200 years before the campaign present. Entire territories, populations, institutions, ecosystems, gods, and ancient sites were stitched into a previously human world. Arriving civilizations already possessed genuine histories. Modern borders have changed during the centuries since.
Native humanity had no physically present gods before the Convergence. Humans also arrived from another world, bringing a separate history.
Gods are physical, vulnerable beings. Mortal institutions have gradually acquired the means to threaten them. Recent Godslayings were undertaken by different organized actors, not one universal movement. When a god dies, its ordinary clerics lose divine spellcasting.
The political imbalance is between powers able to concentrate resources and neighbors whose cooperation remains weak. Divine withdrawal removes protection, mediation, and confidence in older commitments. Geography, trade, ordinary government, and local rivalries remain important independently of the gods.
Ordinary elves can live to about 750 years. No ordinary living elf witnessed the Convergence, but some elders could have personally known survivors. Their states have had ample time to adapt; they are established powers, not newly displaced refugees.
## Living memory of the Convergence
The Convergence, roughly 1,200 years ago, is a living point of reference across the world, comparable in civic and religious weight to a founding, catastrophe, migration, or sacred era. It is not confined to scholarly exposition or pre-Convergence ruins. Families remember through prayers, festivals, old routes, burial grounds, land claims, inherited prejudices, songs, legal charters, architecture, and competing accounts of who lost or gained what. Different cultures name and interpret the event differently. Some date records relative to it, others use older or local calendars; no universal calendar or unanimous story is implied. Descendants of transplanted peoples have lived here for centuries and are not automatically recent refugees, yet labels such as native, arrived, ancestral, and rightful can still shape politics. The wars, migrations, dispossessions, alliances, and reconciliations that followed remain part of ordinary memory.
## Everyday divinity, death, and argument
Gods are part of lived history, not merely remote spell sources. Ordinary people may swear by Tyr, call on Chauntea before planting, keep household shrines, argue over priestly authority, honor dead gods, fear the living ones, or reject divine rule; beliefs and habits vary by settlement, person, occupation, and family. Someone may invoke a god casually without being devout. The deaths of Bane, Lathander, Maglubiyet, Tiamat and more recent deities have distinct public memories, contested interpretations, institutions, pilgrimages, holidays, and grievances. A dead god's ordinary clergy losing spells is socially consequential, but worship, rites, property, charities and communities do not simply vanish. Neither a functioning priest nor a ruler's propaganda automatically proves divine approval. Divine withdrawal and the ability to kill gods inform diplomacy, military ambition, sectarian disputes, and local fears without making every scene about Godslaying.
## Species prejudice and inherited grievances
Species-based prejudice, xenophobia, religious discrimination, nationalism, inherited class privilege, and rival claims of belonging exist in consequential forms. These can mean exclusion from guilds, suspicious searches, unequal inns or rents, marriage-family pressure, discriminatory testimony or officeholding, restrictions on settlement, slurs, harassment, violence, forced assimilation, enslavement, and collective blame after war. Their prevalence, legality, severity, and targets differ by place; they are not evenly universal or an obligatory feature of every encounter. For example, survivors of Dominion incursions may distrust orc or goblinoid travelers regardless of the individual's allegiance; people who know Vhalzareth's slavery may assume every drow supports Lolth; a proud elven house may regard short-lived human claimants as temporary; some human families resent rulers descended from arrivals; outsiders may conflate dragonborn with the dragons their ancestors overthrew. Such judgments can be sincere and wrong, politically cultivated, or personally self-serving. Different communities, families, and members of the same species disagree; victims of prejudice remain individuals with agency. Species does not determine morality, politics or loyalty.
These tensions belong both in practical institutions and in normal talk: trade negotiations, border greetings, religious rites, soldiers' recollections, jokes, family arguments, innkeepers' assumptions, weddings, and rumors. A state may proclaim hospitality while its officers discriminate, or enforce equal standing while neighbors preserve private prejudice. Depict discrimination as a character's or institution's belief and behavior, not as objective DM truth. Include people who resist, complicate, exploit, tolerate, or reproduce it; not everyone is accepting, and not every conversation needs confrontation. Let established history and local incentives determine when it matters, without forcing a moral lesson, mandatory side quest or connection to V's ancestry.
## Spoken lore and the ordinary social baseline
The lore should sound like a culture that people inhabit rather than a historical lecture. NPCs naturally draw on local deities, the Convergence, old migrations, political divisions, saints, divine deaths, rulers, regional heroes and family traditions in their speech when the moment fits. Plausible examples include “Thank Tyr,” “Tyr preserve us,” “By Moradin's forge,” “Convergence take it,” or a wry reference to the Broken Roads; these are illustrative possibilities, not compulsory fixed catchphrases or a universal vocabulary. A person may invoke a deity without being a devoted worshipper, and a person of another faith may reject that invocation. Regional idiom, profession, age, temperament and personal history determine what sounds natural. Use ordinary greetings, jokes, blessings, exasperation and casual remarks more often than exposition.
Across most established mixed cities and busy market towns, ordinary coexistence is the baseline. Residents of different species routinely trade, work, share streets, bargain, form households and participate in civic life; cosmopolitan pragmatism and familiarity usually make overt hostility uncommon. Orsaven, Caereth and Allhaven may contain tensions and unequal institutions, but should not default to universal suspicion, segregated shops or constant racial confrontation. Their own laws and social histories still matter. Local prejudice can persist as assumptions, clumsy humor, selective trust, neighborhood reputation, family expectations or quiet unequal opportunity. Insular districts, contested frontiers, refugee communities, military occupations and religious strongholds can be substantially less welcoming for evidenced reasons; notably the Iron Dominion, Vhalzareth and coercive Veyran institutions retain their established oppressive practices. Urban life is not uniformly tolerant, but neither is bigotry the default personality of every inhabitant.
Occasional species-related ribbing or insulting language may be voiced by believable characters: for example “knife-ears” from someone dismissive of elves, a reciprocal stereotype from an elf, or a familiar joke between people who have an established rapport. Context separates mutually enjoyed teasing, careless prejudice, deliberate insult and actual hatred; other listeners may react differently. Do not scatter slurs or hostile remarks indiscriminately, create a frequency quota, turn every mixed-species interaction into a confrontation, or make all insults equally severe. Deep personal hatred is relatively unusual among ordinary residents and should typically arise from an established experience, family or community memory, inherited local bigotry, war, indoctrination or institution rather than being assigned randomly. A character can nevertheless be mistaken, unfair or hypocritical, and a harmed person need not excuse them. What a prejudiced NPC says expresses that individual's belief, never an objective fact about an entire species. Keep positive, neutral, awkward and hostile interactions proportionate to the actual setting and NPCs, without scripting how V responds.
## Regional voice palette — cities, roads and faith
Adopted regional speech guide for ordinary play (CANON-REGIONAL-VOICES-20261118-01). A location has a locally recognizable mixture of blessings, swear words, jokes, metaphors, nicknames, verbal etiquette and references to its rulers, gods, land, trades and historical memory. These patterns grow from the established cultures below. Quoted phrases are EXAMPLES of plausible speech, not exclusive or compulsory slogans, not proof of a deity's present action, and not scripts every resident must repeat. Select a few appropriate turns of phrase for a particular NPC and use them sparingly; more importantly, let that NPC's outlook, job, upbringing and relationships reflect the same cultural foundation. Dialects vary across neighborhoods, generations, classes, species and professions. Migrants and travelers borrow expressions. Religious speech may invoke multiple gods, household rites, or a deceased god; neither local majority nor a habitual oath determines personal devotion.
Orsaven (Ossent, western Orath): Old royal capital, records, established offices, witnessed promises and inherited crown legitimacy. Tyr, Chauntea, household rites and Lathander's surviving communities all have plausible local voices, without an official single faith. A clerk may say “Tyr witness it” or “Put it before a witness”; a trader jokes “Orsaven will ask for the old seal”; market and river people speak more of barges, grain, and the day's measure than dynastic theory. The old Crownsunder succession is remembered, but not recited by everyone.
Caereth (Crownsunder): Fortified rival capital, household obligations, pledged loyalty, woven bands and a contested throne. “By my household band,” “Tyr judge my word,” and “A promise doesn't die with a king” suit different locals; the last can be political. Frontier families and merchants have coarser road and watch vocabulary than courtiers. The same gods as Ossent are worshipped in overlapping communities; Crownsunder's schism is political, not a universal religious separation. Eastwatch and Rethwyn countryside can favor farm, watch, harvest and road sayings such as “Chauntea spare the late frost”; avoid making one county's idiom the speech of the entire kingdom.
Drusk highlands and Dunrath: Passes, valleys, roads, protected guests, household reputation, winter stores and agreed obligations shape speech. “Keep the road peace,” “A pass keeps account of its travelers,” or “You'll have a roof if someone speaks for you” can be blessing, promise or warning. Local faith varies; don't assign one unestablished patron. A pass-town trader and an isolated valley elder do not speak alike.
Allhaven city: Crossroads and negotiated civic charter encourage mixed-language borrowing, introductions, witness tokens and arguments over who counts as a founding ward. “By the charter,” “You have a place at this table,” and “Road peace, traveler” fit civic voices, sometimes sincerely and sometimes sarcastically. Founding-ward privileges can make a warm welcome coexist with arguments about representation.
Veyrhold and the Veyran Commonwealth (Orath): Formal officials favor Primordial commands, service language, disciplined public declarations and the officially celebrated overthrow of Bane. “No power above the Commonwealth” reflects established doctrine; an officer may demand “State your service,” while an exhausted citizen may mutter about another inspection. A few people remember Bane with dread, gratitude for his fall, or forbidden nostalgia; they do not normally praise him as a living patron. State rhetoric is not every citizen's private speech; occupied Dunwall retains its own charter-language and remembers its recent loss.
Kheldorn and Harrowdelve (Arden): Moradin's active defense, the Ironspine, rescue obligations, masonry and frontier kin losses give expressions such as “Moradin steady the stone,” “Mind the bridge,” or “Leave no neighbor below.” A frightened frontier refugee is likely to speak more sharply about Dominion troops than a sheltered workshop apprentice.
Aundar and Embercrown: Forge, stewardship, inherited work, sheltered Ambercrags valleys and clan duty shape “By Moradin's forge,” “A well-kept work outlives its maker,” and arguments about who earned the right to sign a craft. Talveren and Kithring differ: seven founding holds, compact readings, connecting roads and independent infrastructure favor “Seven holds, seven answers” and “Keep the road sound.” Neither is simply a duplicate of Kheldorn's besieged frontier speech.
Ilvaran and Ilarath (high elves): Long apprenticeships, controlled valleys, scholarly lineage, old households and Corellon's absence influence measured formal expressions such as “Let the record outlast us,” “By the long reckoning,” or a private wish for Corellon's return. Elves vary in patience and humor; not all speak like nobles or have personally witnessed the Convergence.
Elderweald and Thalanor (wood elves): Managed forest, negotiated paths, returning travelers, hunting restraint and seasonal rites inform “Leave a path for the returning,” “The path remembers,” and invocations associated with Rillifane or Solonor. Those gods' current survival is not established. Non-elven villages with inherited forest rights have their own local variants.
Vhalzareth (drow): Lolth's priesthood can invoke “May Lolth watch” as blessing, assertion or threat; noble houses use layered politeness, rank and dangerous courtesy. Workers, traders, enslaved people and dissidents have other, often guarded vocabularies. A phrase spoken respectfully at a house audience need not be sincere at a kitchen table. Their society is oppressive; drow individuals are not interchangeable.
Open Table Accord (eastern Namarra): Yondalla and Garl Glittergold are missing but their clergy still receive spells; ordinary people may still invoke them. Honeymeads favors hearth, bees and shared stores (“Yondalla keep a plate”). Crooked Bridges uses bridges, crossings and trading introductions (“Count the spans before the bargain”). Unbarred March favors unclosed roads and common rights. Bellwether Coast favors the harbor bell and fog warnings (“Hear the bell before you sail”). Glass Warrens draws jokes from measures, glass and repair (“Garl spare this glass”). Wandering Wood favors guided paths and invitations (“A path isn't owed to strangers”). These are six distinguishable cultures, not one interchangeable halfling/gnome accent.
Zafira and Oathmark (southern Arden): Shared Serevan headwaters, irrigation, flood relief, annual water reckoning and arguments between upstream and downstream interests inform “May the first water come gently,” “Ask upstream before you promise downstream,” and seasonal jokes about who gets the mud. Local household gods and faiths remain varied; do not invent exclusive divine patronage.
Talassar and Charter Coast ports (Arden and Namarra): Sailors, pilots, salvagers and merchants share practical sea sayings but disagree over taxes and maritime independence. “Rescue before recovery,” “Leave a berth for the stranded,” “The unanswered bell tolls for everyone,” and fearful references to Umberlee or navigation blessings invoking Selûne are appropriate where personal faith and trade fit. These gods' current status is not fixed by the saying. Talaris court speech differs from Veyport's republican harbor bargaining and Merovar's seafaring merchant houses.
Lastlight and Dawnrest (Namarra): Lathander died about three centuries ago, but refuge, memorial, rebuilding and dawn observances continue. “By Lathander's last light,” “First a meal, then questions,” and “Let the dawn remember them” can signal hope, grief or sometimes criticism when hospitality fails. Such invocations do not imply ordinary Lathander clerics still receive spells.
Dragonborn Vathrune and the five custodial city-states: Draconic, liberation memory, the Day of Open Gates, five-wick lamps and Tiamat's tomb inform “Keep the gates open,” “Five flames, none at the center,” and respectful or bitter references to the captive Great Wyrms. Pilgrims, civic officials and custodians use different formulas. Not all venerate Tiamat. Neither these sayings nor ordinary dragonborn boasting implies willingness or ability to release or control a Great Wyrm; an explicit release threat remains exceptional and existential only.
Iron Dominion and Gorvath (Arden): Gruumsh's priesthood and loyal military officers may say “Under Gruumsh's eye”; founding clans recall oaths, names, hearths and conquest in differing forms. Goblin and hobgoblin households have their own speech and contested memories of Maglubiyet, including sincere Gruumsh devotion, mourning and rejection. Conquered communities may invoke their older gods privately. Do not give every orc one voice or transplant this idiom into the Veyran theater.
## Regional conversational registers and class variation
The regional voice palette above supplies cultural references; this section supplies how conversations unfold. Regional speech is an interaction pattern, not an accent gimmick or a list of obligatory oaths. For any speaker combine: (1) local norms for greeting, respect, disagreement, teasing, apology and promises; (2) social register from occupation, class, education and formal versus intimate company; (3) the individual's rhythm and personality. Distinguish ordinary short forms and street idioms from formal prayers, laws or courtly maxims. A deity's name may be an oath rather than a profession of faith. Avoid phonetic caricature, universal slang, repetitive catchphrases or artificially verbose historical allusions. The examples below are usable defaults for future NPCs, NOT retroactively played quotations or immutable rules about every resident.
Orsaven and Ossent: Officials and aristocratic households favor complete names, measured statements, careful distinctions between what was witnessed and what is merely alleged; a senior clerk can be chilly without being rude. River crews, laborers and market brokers are faster, interrupt over prices, and use seals, measures, barges and crossings for metaphors; “show me the mark,” “fine for the ledger, not for the boat,” and “that's royal ink” may signal doubt. A courtier's indirect disagreement (“I should want a witness to that”) contrasts with a carter's “Not for that price.” Tyr and Chauntea enter mundane speech without one mandatory religion.
Caereth, Crownsunder, and Rethwyn countryside: Noble/formal speakers emphasize whom they have pledged to, family standing and hospitality; they may challenge a slight through courteous obligation rather than a blunt accusation. Guards, stable hands and hill farmers speak in shorter practical commitments about watch, road, fleece, weather and hearth; “hold your end,” “you've my word,” or “frost before seed” suit some. Rethwyn staff sound like working rural households, not the court. Under grief, courtly composure may fracture; a person does not recite the succession dispute at every meal.
Drusk and Dunrath: Guests are introduced through household and route rather than just titles; people may test whether someone can be vouched for. Highland elders speak with deliberate pauses over obligations and stores; pass guides use quick warnings and clipped directions (“road peace,” “winter road,” “mind the scree”). A well-traveled pass trader is brisker and less ceremonial than a secluded valley leader. No single required patron god.
Allhaven: Mixed-ward speakers borrow trade terms freely and expect introductions, reciprocal explanation, and public civility. A charter magistrate frames disagreements as whose rights are at stake; a warehouse worker may say “Who's standing for that promise?” or “Take your place, then pay your share.” “By the charter” may be genuine, ironic or weary depending on status. Newer districts may speak about unequal representation more sharply than founding wards.
Veyrhold, the Commonwealth and occupied Dunwall: Official public speech is direct, repetitive about service and approval, and careful about political implications; questions may sound like loyalty tests. Private Veyran citizens can be sardonic, affectionate, frightened or guarded, often shifting register when an officer enters. “Put it on the service sheet” may be a joke as well as a threat. Dunwall residents retain their charter talk and older local pride; they need not sound like the occupier. Official Primordial and practical Common use differ by context; the Dominion is a separate distant theater.
Kheldorn/Harrowdelve versus Aundar/Embercrown versus Talveren/Kithring: Kheldorn's frontline households prefer immediate concrete warnings, witnessed rescue promises and blunt repair assessments; gallows humor can follow genuine loss. Aundar's craft households take longer to judge workmanship, ask whose master taught a technique, and use restrained correction rather than blanket insult. Talveren's corridor merchants and engineers argue swiftly from shared maintenance obligations and negotiated compromise, sometimes teasing each other about the seven holds. All three may invoke Moradin but must not have identical rhythms.
Ilvaran/Ilarath, Elderweald/Thalanor, and Vhalzareth: Ilvaran formal speakers may qualify claims, leave thoughtful pauses and avoid an irreversible statement; young travelers can be impatient and colloquial. Elderweald negotiations may begin with permission, who used the path before, and what can be returned; practical hunters may be blunt and economical. Vhalzareth noble exchanges use carefully ranked compliments and implied consequences; workers and dissidents can be direct in trusted private rooms yet guarded under observation. An elf's age or drow ancestry alone does not predetermine their behavior or politics.
The Open Table Accord: Honeymeads family talk uses warm offers of food and gently sharp teasing; Crooked Bridges bargaining is lively, transactional and full of interruptions about spans, ferries and introductions. Unbarred March residents favor blunt reciprocal rights and public-road promises; Bellwether sailors compress warnings into practical calls. Glass Warrens specialists ask for demonstration, exact measures and witty corrections; Wandering Wood residents favor indirect guidance and deliberate invitations. These differences matter more than replacing every sentence with “Yondalla” or “Garl.”
Zafira/Oathmark: Downstream merchants may insist on dates, flows, shares and practical repayment, while upland speakers start with who cleared the headwaters, maintained the banks and sheltered neighbors. Dry humor about mud, drought and upstream promises crosses their boundary. Neither state has a single speech register or exclusive patron god.
Talassar/Charter Coast harbors: Deck crews speak in concise warnings and weather/rigging terms; pilots speak decisively when lives depend on it. Talassari courtiers are more deferential to crown and naval rank, while Charter Coast merchants argue shares, compensation and freedom of trade more openly. Sea oaths may invoke Umberlee in fear or Selûne for navigation without proving personal devotion; a dockworker's profane joke and a memorial bell are different registers.
Lastlight/Dawnrest: Refuge workers often lead with a concrete offer of food or shelter, then ask questions; temple keepers and veterans can use measured memorial language. A person who has exhausted their charity may speak curtly or criticize lofty promises. References to Lathander honor a dead god without implying that his ordinary clergy still cast spells.
Vathrune and the dragonborn states: Pilgrims may speak through rituals of remembrance; civic traders prefer contracts and harbor arrangements; custodial professionals keep language precise and controlled when discussing dangerous captivity. Jokes about Five Flames can circulate privately, but threats to unleash a Great Wyrm are not normal bargaining language. A diplomat's restraint should not imply command over a captive.
Iron Dominion/Gorvath: Gruumsh-loyal officers and priests use hierarchy, vows, commands and triumphal claims; founding clan elders invoke hearths, fallen kin and witnessed contributions. Ordinary goblin, hobgoblin, orc and conquered-subject households have varied workmanlike language and private doubts, not a uniform brutal growl. Propaganda does not define the individual's personal register.
Use speech length and interruptions to reflect situation: a frightened guard may drop politeness; a bereaved noble may lose composure; a warm friend may be terse under pressure. Characters can deliberately code-switch for a stranger, employer, lover or superior. In crowded multiethnic cities ordinary civility remains the baseline, while prejudice, if present, arises from a specific individual and context. When a new local saying is repeatedly established through play, preserve it in this world owner; when one NPC's voice changes through experience, preserve the change in that NPC's record.
## Neighboring societies and everyday political memory
Adopted social texture and conversation reference (CANON-NEIGHBOR-SOCIAL-20261118-01). History, geography, faith, language, livelihoods and proximity determine what an ordinary person notices. These examples refine how already established relationships appear in speech, commerce, hospitality and disagreement; they do NOT declare a new treaty, war, shared border, troop movement, popular consensus, or encounter. “Neighbor” can mean directly contested border, connected overland district or practical sea-market partner; do not turn a trade relationship into adjacent sovereign territory. People sharing a frontier often have more in common with each other than with distant capitals, while disagreeing intensely about a particular claim. Differences should shape bargaining and small remarks, not require every meeting to discuss a war.
Ossent / Crownsunder — one inherited cultural region divided by a roughly 80-year-old succession war. Shared Tyr/Chauntea household traditions, woven bands, market and river trades, marriage connections and remembrance of dead on both sides make many cross-border encounters familiar. Ossent's crown does not recognize Crownsunder's independence; Crownsunder claims Ossent's throne. An Orsaven official may insist upon lawful archives and charters; a Caereth household retainer may appeal to hereditary oath and recognized succession. Cross-border traders can tease one another about “which Crown,” ask whether their old contacts are safe, or avoid the topic to make a sale. Border landowners, displaced heirs and former fighters' families may be genuinely bitter. Neither population uniformly hates the other, and the current armistice, restricted trade and frontier raids do not imply total war.
Crownsunder / Veyran theater and Ossent beyond — proximity to Veyr's expansion makes eastern Crownsunder households more alert to military news, border defenses, permits, troop provisioning and protection than distant western farmers. Ossent and Crownsunder exchange warnings indirectly but still disagree about the throne, delaying dependable cooperation. Someone may be furious at a rival dynasty and still fear the Commonwealth more. Do not establish an unplayed invasion or claim every Orath village is under immediate attack. Veyr's threat is separate from Gruumsh's distant Arden war.
Drusk / Allhaven / Dunwall and Veyr — Drusk's pass-town households speak of road peace, witnesses, whom a guest answers to and keeping winter routes open; Allhaven's mixed neighborhoods speak of charter protections and the gap between founding wards and newer districts. Both depend on highland-to-lowland roads and Dornal commerce. Dunwall's recent Veyran occupation, after inadequate neighboring aid, gives nearby officials, river shippers and refugees reasons to argue about promises, crossings, taxes, fear and responsibility. Some resent the delay; others defend a hard choice or fear reprisals. These are available differing attitudes, not established statements by every resident. Dunwall's resistance remains embryonic and its suspended charter is not already restored.
Ilvaran and western Orath's human approaches — contact through controlled valleys, craft, scholarship and trade can coexist with Ilvaran's guarded settlement rights and slow institutional decisions. A human trader may joke that an elven petition outlives a grandparent; an elven house may worry that quick settlements ignore graves and inheritance. A longstanding immigrant family can be trusted where an unknown visitor is not. The exact direct national border/roads are not fixed; describe practical approaches without inventing a mapped crossing.
Kheldorn / Iron Dominion — immediate Arden frontier: Moradin's defensive skirmishes, raids, lost holdings and displaced households make warnings, watch rotations, rescued relatives and provisioning practical concerns. Hatred of specific imperial troops or commanders can be earned by violence; suspicion toward an independent orc or goblin merchant may be unfair and contested, not universal. The Dominion's conquest and engineered demographic displacement are real state policies; individual orcs and goblinoids, including those outside its rule, have separate loyalties. No full invasion or siege of the great dwarven holds is established.
Kheldorn / Talveren / Aundar — a connected dwarven corridor, not one kingdom or instantaneous neighbor-to-neighbor response. Shared Moradin traditions, clan ties, travel commerce and responsibilities for long-lived works create affectionate rivalry about craftsmanship, priorities and who pays for repairs. Kheldorn's exposed defenders may demand immediate help; Talveren's independently founded holds may resist assumed allegiance; Aundar's court may insist on its own commitments and resources. Travel takes meaningful time; a merchant hearing of a pass fight does not mean Aundar's army already arrived.
Zafira / Oathmark — former common territory with recognized separate government but continuing disputes over upstream water, downstream markets, navigational dues, flood warnings, timber and defense burdens. A boatmaster may praise Oathmark's early warning and complain about its diversion in the next breath. Oathmark has a southern coast and independent overseas trade, not absolute dependence on Zafiran ports. A season's water disagreement is not automatically a battle, and river users may cooperate where rulers quarrel.
Talassar / Charter Coast — separation recognized after maritime tax and trade disputes, with heavy continuing commerce and contested compensation/naval obligations. Shared pilotage, storm rescue, absent shares and harbor memorials make a Talassari sailor and a Charter Coast shipwright culturally familiar. They may quarrel over tariffs, naval duty and which port can be trusted while still rescuing each other's crews. Talaris royal court, Merovar merchants, Veyport assembly interests and Selwick harbor politics have different registers, not one permanent personal feud.
Lastlight / nearby Talassari and Charter Coast sea partners — refugee passage, food, shipping, temple memory and contested obligations to shelter people matter. Lastlight's promise of a first meal can be honored by a local attendant yet challenged over overcrowded stores. A port captain may prefer payment or safe sailing without despising the refugees. These are sea connections, not contiguous land borders; religion survives Lathander's death without ordinary clerical spellcasting from him.
The Open Table Accord / Elderweald and internal Accord neighbors — eastern Namarra's six sovereign member states share cooperation, trade, hospitality and friction over water, roads, unequal defensive contributions and local land rights. Their daily cultures remain distinct. On the Elderweald approaches, known non-elven villages may have inherited privileges while unknown outsiders—including elves—must negotiate. Use the specific forest use-rights and paths at issue; don't invent a war or assume the six members vote alike. Each state's existing named customs still prevail.
Surface elven courts / Vhalzareth — shared distant history and an uneasy limited accommodation over envoys and selected trade, NOT adjacency or a unified elven alliance. Outsiders can oppose Lolthite slavery without assuming all drow support it. A surface elf may remember the Crown Wars through tradition but has not personally witnessed the Convergence; a drow exile may have views opposite the priesthood. Underdark towns such as Durnak Hold, Hushvaults and Latch have distinct direct local relations with Vhalzareth; their underground routes do not create a shared surface frontier.
Dragonborn custodian states / surrounding ports — Vathrune's pilgrimage and the five dispersed city-states connect by difficult sea journeys, compact institutions and contested custody burdens. Foreign traders can fear dragons, misunderstand dragonborn liberation, or make insensitive jokes. The five captive Great Wyrms are uncontrolled existential last-resort deterrents for their individual states, not ordinary diplomatic threats, trade leverage or readily coordinated bombardments. A distant port's rumor about a prison is not proof of a release or immediate attack.
## Local knowledge, news travel and political salience
Before an NPC comments on politics or a conflict, anchor (1) what the person could know, (2) how that information could have arrived by the current campaign day, (3) what they believe versus what is confirmed, and (4) why it matters to their work, faith, home or relationships. Direct witnesses know their own experience, not an army's entire plan. Border guards may know patrol changes; merchants tariffs, cargo shortages and road delays; priests worship, refugees and congregational concerns; scholars older records; nobles contested claims and court rumors; port pilots arrivals and maritime hazards. Access depends on routes, literacy, class, contacts and censorship. A well-informed specialist may know more than a neighboring farmer, but no ordinary NPC is an omniscient newsreader.
Use the established courier partnerships, ships, inns, temples, itinerant people, expeditions and occasional scarce magic. Nearby exchanges can be quick, inland and overseas accounts delayed, incomplete or distorted; the Dominion heartland and Veyr lie months apart by normal travel. Do not assign exact news dates unless confirmed in play or owning records. On receiving new evidence, distinguish witnessed fact, official proclamation, partisan telling, hearsay and speculation; a false rumor can matter without becoming objective truth. A city's conversation may lag behind events outside it. Realm-wide knowledge, faction decisions, new attacks, cross-border cooperation, and NPC messages require actual established causes and travel/contact time.
Most people prioritize immediate lives: prices, harvest, funerals, routes, family, employment, local safety and religious observance. International affairs become conversation when relevant, not by a requirement to mention empires each scene. A Kheldorn frontier family can be consumed by Dominion raids while an Orsaven miller scarcely thinks of Gruumsh; Dunwall's occupation may affect a nearby Dornal boatman more than a far-off coastal sailor. Never merge the Arden Dominion and Orath Commonwealth into one coordinated war.
## Species, nationality, origin and individual loyalty
Keep four separate axes for NPCs and communities: species/ancestry (a person's physical descent), civic membership or nationality (whose laws and obligations apply), upbringing/learned local culture (speech, food, faith, manners and social ties), and personal loyalty or convictions (what they actually choose). A fifth useful axis is current community, employment and class. The axes can align but need not. A long-settled orc citizen of Allhaven need not admire Gruumsh; a human raised in a dwarven hold may share its manners; a drow abroad need not worship Lolth; a Veyran dissenter may speak Primordial while rejecting the regime. These are illustrative social possibilities, not previously established particular NPCs or a blanket grant of local citizenship.
The world does not permit ordinary cross-species biological offspring (subject to the single established exception involving V's original species), but marriage, adoption, chosen kinship, migration, learning and cultural membership remain possible. Family customs can travel with migrants for generations, and mixed cities are usually accustomed to everyday coexistence. An inherited surname, skin, ears, language or religious invocation does not prove birthplace, allegiance, citizenship, beliefs, or knowledge. Never assign automatic alignment, fanaticism, prejudice or expected loyalties by species.
Prejudice exists as occasional joking, stereotypes, suspicion, systemic exclusion or, less commonly among ordinary urban residents, profound personal hostility. Its severity follows local history, social institutions, trauma or ideology, not a universal species reaction. An NPC can unfairly generalize from actual harm; another can challenge that view while sharing fear of the offending government. Established oppressive practices in the Dominion, Vhalzareth and Veyr must remain real, without treating all their subjects as villains. Let the affected people have independent priorities and agency. Respect the speaker's actual history, familiarity, status and relationship; do not make all foreigners misunderstood, all citizens tolerant, or every conversation a prejudice incident.
## Cultural decisions, daily faith and visible Convergence layers
Adopted cultural expression guidance (ADMIN-CULTURE-PASS-20261118-01). A cultural distinction is real when it changes what people do, pay for, remember, refuse, honor, fear or consider polite, not only the words they say. These are broad patterns grounded in established state institutions and practices; not every inhabitant believes in them. An NPC's role, household, circumstances and personal commitments decide what matters. In scenes prefer one concrete consequential behavior over several decorative catchphrases. Do not infer automatic checks, hostile scenes, new decrees, hidden sites, paid contracts or events from a cultural example.
Cultural choice contrasts:
- Ossent: institutional recognition, reliable witnesses, inherited royal offices, archival continuity and contracted commerce can make a clerk insist on documented authority while an Orsaven merchant prioritizes trustworthy delivery. Crownsunder: personal oaths, household obligations and recognition of hereditary succession can make a retainer uphold a promise even when it is commercially inconvenient. Both sides share family rites, hospitality and law; either can also abuse them. A cross-border family may value its relatives more than either crown.
- Kheldorn: people who have experienced frontier raids may prioritize rescue, watch rotations, timely readiness and Moradin's defense. Aundar: a master may stress proper stewardship and workmanship. Talveren: a council delegate may insist one hold not conscript another without agreement. The same dwarven background does not dictate one policy.
- Drusk: a host may ask who will answer for a traveler and offer protected lodging after an introduction. Allhaven: a citizen may cite charter rights and argue about founding-ward privilege, even while providing everyday hospitality. Dunwall: an occupied resident may comply in public while privately resenting the suspension of the city's charter. Veyran officers may demand official service; individual Veyrans can dissent.
- Zafira and Oathmark: farmers, boatmen and officials may behave differently during flood warning, water allocation or maintenance season; upstream labor and downstream consequences each matter. Talassar and the Charter Coast: rescuing people before claiming salvage and honoring absent crew shares are moral norms that can clash with private profit and disputed treaty obligations.
- Lastlight: a refuge attendant might offer immediate food before arguing about long-term supplies; dissenters may sincerely question how far the obligation extends. In the Accord a Honeymeads neighbor may preserve a spare plate, a Crooked Bridges merchant may honor a bridge introduction, and a Wandering Wood guide may insist on permission before an unfamiliar path is taken. Elderweald forest-rights agreements may protect familiar non-elven residents while requiring newcomers to negotiate; Ilvaran's elite inheritance and lengthy deliberations can frustrate younger households.
- Vhalzareth's oppressive priesthood and slavery determine many institutional choices, but individual drow, free workers, enslaved people and dissenters have differing priorities and loyalties. The Iron Dominion's engineered displacement and brutal settlement are policies rather than universal orc dispositions. Dragonborn city-states' custody and liberation traditions can affect guard discipline, memorials and external agreements without implying controllable Great Wyrms or casual nuclear threats.
Everyday religious practice and disagreement:
- Gods appear through household oaths, blessings, funerals, work rituals, alms, public feasts, apprenticeships and shrines, not constant spectacular divine visits. Most people can honor several gods or inherited customs. A rural Crownsunder household may ask Chauntea's favor over seed, a witness may invoke Tyr's justice, a Kheldorn rescuer Moradin, a sailor may appease Umberlee or ask Selûne for bearings. These are plausible practices, NOT mandatory local devotion or proof of a deity's present activity.
- Faith shapes decisions when a particular person takes it seriously: a devotee of Tyr may favor truthful testimony over an advantageous lie; a priest may argue that protecting a neighbor is the greater justice. Two worshippers of Tyr may reasonably disagree about retaliation, mercy or a ruler's legitimacy. Priests, lay household attendants and spellcasting clergy have distinct authority. Religion may conflict with employment, kin loyalty or fear.
- Dead gods still matter. Lastlight's remembrance of Lathander, Vathrune's tomb rites for Tiamat, Veyr's memory of Bane's overthrow, and goblinoid divisions over Maglubiyet survive without ordinary cleric spellcasting from a dead god. No service, phrase or traditional religious office automatically proves the god is alive, speaking or restoring magic. Functions and locations of surviving or missing gods remain as already established.
- An existing playable anchor: Iven Marlowe asked for an ordinary modest burial with prayer to Tyr and bread shared among tenants, not a lordly mausoleum. This is player-known preference from the current investigation; it establishes his own values, not a universal Rethwyn burial mandate. Do not deliver his private messages, arrange the funeral or change the current scene through this OOC cultural guidance.
Physical inheritance of the Convergence:
- After about 1,200 years, historical seams are often ordinary working landscapes, repaired roads, mixed construction methods and old settlement memories. Old foundation courses can diverge from later streets; reused stones and transplanted inscriptions can appear in walls; a place-name can survive after a road or river changed. Household heirlooms and inherited prayer formulas may refer to the world a family originally came from. Such details should be coherent with established geography, age, local settlement and actual known evidence. They are not random anomalies, automatic magic, or compulsory mysteries.
- Orsaven is an old native royal capital with surviving institutional records; its setting can show old and later construction beside functioning trade, not an unsurveyed continent-wide Scarland. Caereth and surrounding Crownsunder communities inherit the same older regional culture and a much younger political schism; postwar bands and disputed memorials may matter more visibly than the distant Convergence. Across Arden the abrupt Ironspine seam is geographically established; Namarra's shorelines and some underground systems retain transformed history; dragonborn temples and Vathrune preserve older memory. Do not assert a particular pre-Convergence site, anomalous property, relic, archaeological find, street name, road connection or date in the party's current scene without prior evidence/appropriate scene establishment.
- Use a visible material trace when fitting: a stone of a different building tradition in a repaired market wall, a preserved older inscription, a family rite, a pilgrim's remembered route, a disputed foundation marker or architecture appropriate to a district. Choose one with a mundane history and consequence rather than attaching a clue, compulsory treasure or plot to everything. Distinguish established map/place canon from illustrative possible set dressing; adopt a specific new location or object only when it is actually established in play or explicitly adopted as local canon.
Design constraints: ordinary town life remains generally mixed and civil; racism is not a mandatory encounter beat. Let urban class and occupation matter as much as ancestry. Locality determines a population's proximity to each war, not a universal global emergency. This guidance expands daily behavior and scenery, not the cast, secret plot, historic events, gameplay or treasure.
Deployment in play: For a new settlement, first identify actual local geography, founding history, customary gods/rites, work, languages, social disputes and contact with foreigners; establish two or three useful speech habits and one local reference before recurring NPC conversations, saving only durable adopted facts in their owning world/NPC record. For important individual NPCs, preserve an idiolect across scenes rather than cycling through every local expression. Distinguish everyday common speech from religious ceremony, political slogan, joke, insult and formal oath; speak naturally, rarely explain the idiom unless V asks, and never force V to repeat or respond. No quotas, comedy accents, invented universal prejudices, or retroactive rewording of played dialogue.

# Souls religion and public knowledge
Souls and the afterlife exist independently of the gods and predate their arrival. The Convergence joined previously separate routes into the afterlife. Gods can guide, protect, influence, or capture souls but do not automatically own worshippers. A patron's death does not destroy the afterlife or automatically condemn followers.
Resurrection can return willing, available souls when a capable caster and resources exist, subject to the campaign revival rule recorded in the Continuity Pack's `RULES` tab. Check the applicable 2014 effect for spell-specific eligibility, timing, and costs; flag any unresolved interaction with the house rule before resolving it. Ghosts can remain through attachment or interference. The ultimate afterlife, Kelemvor's role, and whether gods have mortal-like souls remain unresolved.
Funerals honor the dead, help souls depart, and protect remains. Most require no spellcasting. Families and ordinary attendants preserve meaningful traditions after a god dies.
Faiths cross borders and extend beyond their most prominent state or order. Divine wishes, religious institutions, and political claims can differ. Tyr's faith is not synonymous with the Wardens; Moradin and Lathander also have followers beyond the states most associated with them.
## Worship and religious office
Most people honor several gods according to circumstance, alongside household traditions and particular patron devotion. Households maintain shrines, prayers, remembrance, and offerings around births, marriages, departures, and deaths. Work and travel prompt observances relevant to livelihoods and dangers. Community festivals combine worship, markets, shared meals, repairs, and remembrance. Personal patronage does not necessarily exclude other deities.
Physical divine presence does not make ordinary worship a personal audience. Most religious life passes through local institutions and inherited customs. Meaningful rites often require no magic. Religious office and spellcasting are separate: most people maintaining religious life have no divine magic, and a person with divine magic need not control a temple.
| Role | Function |
| - | - |
| Household attendants and elders | Preserve family observances, teach prayers, and conduct ordinary rites |
| Local priests | Lead worship, funerals, festivals, teaching, and care; many cannot cast spells |
| Divinely empowered clergy | Provide scarce magical services alongside religious duties; availability varies |
| Custodians and administrators | Maintain sacred places, records, stores, property, and hospitality |
| Itinerant religious figures | Carry teachings and news and connect underserved communities to distant congregations |
Faiths assign authority through differing combinations of learning, appointment, inheritance, community recognition, and demonstrated blessings. A god's death ends ordinary clerical spellcasting but not ceremonies, schools, charity, property, or religious offices.
## Knowledge and divine attention
Major gods have witnesses, records, and demonstrated activity, but distant accounts can be distorted. Continued clerical spellcasting is strong evidence that a patron survives; its absence in one priest proves little. Direct commands exist, yet clergy and rulers can interpret, selectively report, or fabricate them. Real favor does not validate every claim made by its recipient. Disasters invite competing explanations involving ordinary causes, divine action, curses, and political interests.
| Means of seeking attention | Expectation |
| - | - |
| Prayer and offerings | Express devotion, gratitude, fear, or requests without guaranteed answers |
| Temple petitions | Ask clergy to intercede, investigate, or arrange access |
| Pilgrimage | Reach a sacred place, fulfill obligations, or seek an audience; arrival guarantees neither admission nor help |
| Divination | Obtain limited answers where suitable practitioners and magic are available |
| Direct audience | Exceptional access shaped by temperament, location, attendants, and political interests |
Prayer does not make a god omniscient. Particular invocations may be perceived through individual capabilities, but not every prayer is established to reach its recipient. Silence does not establish refusal. Worship, attention, and persuasion remain distinct. Exact divine senses and individual capabilities remain open.
## Shared communities and temple support
Congregations cooperate and compete. Families can combine household and multiple-faith funeral traditions. Temples negotiate festival dates, markets, processions, and expenses; cooperate in relief while disputing resources or conditions; share springs, burial grounds, and sanctuaries under local customs; and compete over schools, property, appointments, and influence.
Acknowledging a deity does not require moral approval or devotion. Offerings made in fear need not imply acceptance of its teachings. Groups can acknowledge the same god while disagreeing over obligations or whether its demands deserve obedience. Lolth suppresses competing allegiance, Gruumsh demands submission, and Veyr subjects religion to Commonwealth authority. Elsewhere negotiated coexistence need not provide equal wealth or influence.
| Temple support | Benefit and tension |
| - | - |
| Offerings and communal labor | Maintain local rites and buildings but can burden poorer households |
| Land and productive property | Sustain personnel, hospitality, and relief; temples can be demanding landlords |
| Patronage | Funds major works while donors seek influence, appointments, or prestige |
| Endowments | Preserve schools, rites, and charitable duties even after circumstances change |
| Specialist service payments | Support skilled work and scarce magical resources; subsidies determine access for those unable to pay |
Ordinary worship need not require payment. A small shrine can depend on shared upkeep while a wealthy sanctuary maintains estates, workshops, and guesthouses. Temples retain property and obligations after divine death, prompting disputes among clergy, congregations, rulers, and heirs.
## Responses to divine death
Followers can preserve teachings and ceremonies, seek restoration through remains or fragments, adopt another patron, reject the former deity, or contest its legacy. Responses coexist and divide communities. Changing patron does not automatically renew spellcasting. Lastlight, Vathrune, Veyr, and Maglubiyet's former followers embody different histories of divine loss.
Fragments do not automatically replace the dead deity. Possession grants neither authority over followers nor restoration of ordinary clerical spells. Divine resurrection and any fragment-based substitute for patronage remain unresolved. Additional gods' presence and survival remain open where not already established, preserving choices relevant to the PCs.
# Divine fragment knowledge and rights
Fragments are widely known but poorly understood. Ordinary people know stories; officials and clergy recognize signs worth investigating; trained specialists use investigation, tools and rituals without guaranteed immediate identification. Fraud is common. Confirmation attracts competing owners, officials, scholars, buyers and thieves.
Fragments need not be obvious, portable, or usable. Killing a host does not automatically transfer its power. The demigod PCs' fragment-triggered reawakening is a campaign progression rule recorded in the Continuity Pack's `RULES` tab.
Existing property rights, land rights, salvage customs, heritage claims and local strategic-material laws govern finds. Finding something alone does not establish ownership. Landscape fragments raise public access and community claims; a fragment in a person does not make that person property, although oppressive governments may violate the principle.
The Dominion seizes fragments; Veyr requisitions strategic discoveries; Accord members retain local laws; Allhaven hears competing claims in courts; Lastlight's custodians assert special responsibility for Lathander-associated remnants. Wardens have no universal confiscation right.
